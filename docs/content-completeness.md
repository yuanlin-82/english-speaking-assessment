# Content completeness (why this axis exists)

Field / product note. **Not** production rubrics or vendor internals.

---

## What the axis is

**Content completeness** answers: *did this response cover the informational / communicative demand of the item?*

It is **task coverage**—not CAF **complexity**, not depth of argument, and not “integrity” in the moral sense. See also [construct.md](construct.md).

---

## Why it is a compromise

Fully open speaking is hard to **auto-score** with current scoring technology. Depth, nuance, and free argument structure do not map cleanly onto automated pipelines that need stable targets or constrained answer spaces.

Among widely recognized large-scale English speaking exams (for example IELTS and TOEFL as *familiar cases*, not as product claims):

- Judging **content depth / complexity** still largely depends on **professional human examiners**.
- Formats that are **fully automated** keep answers from being too free: item design **constrains** what a scorable response can look like.

So a hiring oral report that includes **content completeness** under automation is accepting an operational trade-off: score what the machine can check (coverage vs task demand / reference), not what a trained examiner would score as rich topic development.

---

## How task design carries the compromise

Item types lean on known targets or bounded meaning (read-aloud, listen–repeat, paraphrase, listen–answer, semi-open expression)—see [task-design.md](task-design.md). The more free the speech, the weaker automatic content scoring becomes; the battery stays inside that limit on purpose.

---

## Takeaways

1. Content completeness = **coverage of task demand**, not depth / complexity.  
2. Auto-scoring open content remains limited; large exams still use examiners for depth-like judgments.  
3. Automatable oral batteries **constrain answer freedom** in design—this axis is that compromise, named honestly.
